# The Problems of Philosophy, Reconsidered
## A Field Guide to Systematic Philosophical Analysis

*A comprehensive successor to Bertrand Russell's 1912 introduction — retaining its clarity and its conviction that philosophy is worth doing, while repairing its gaps in philosophy of mind, language, science, value, and method.*

---

## Design of the Book

**Thesis of the book:** Philosophy is not a catalogue of opinions but a *discipline of systematic analysis* — the disciplined study of what we would have to show, say, and assume in order to make our ordinary beliefs, concepts, and practices coherent. The book therefore does two things at once: it surveys the great problems, and it teaches the reader *how to work on any problem* — how to isolate a question, construct and dismantle arguments, apply distinctions, and know when a question has been answered, dissolved, or merely renamed.

**Who it is for:** Undergraduate and graduate students; general readers who want a single, honest map of the terrain; working philosophers in one area who want a reliable guide to the others.

## PART I — THE METHOD: HOW TO DO PHILOSOPHY
*The tools before the terrain.*

### Chapter 1 — What Philosophy Is, and What It Is For
1. The naive picture: philosophy as opinion, and why it fails.
2. Three working definitions: philosophy as *conceptual analysis*, as *rational reconstruction*, as *the study of the conditions of intelligibility*.
3. The perennial questions and the "permanent problems" illusion — what changes and what stays.
4. Philosophy and its neighbours: where science, logic, art, and religion hand off to philosophy and back.
5. The *payoff* problem: why answers are not the only product; clarity, vision, and the escape from parochialism.
6. Case studies: the same question (e.g., "What is justice?", "What is a cause?") across four centuries.

### Chapter 2 — The Anatomy of an Argument
1. Assertions, questions, and the difference between a question and a pseudo-question.
2. Validity, soundness, deductive vs. ampliative inference; the `premise–conclusion` architecture.
3. Hidden premises and suppressed assumptions; the *principle of charity* and its limits.
4. Argument maps, dialectical trees, and the technique of *steel-manning*.
5. Explanation vs. argument: *explanans*, *explanandum*, and the inference to the best explanation.
6. Workshop: reconstructing three classic arguments — the ontological argument, the argument from illusion, the is–ought gap.

### Chapter 3 — The Instruments of Analysis
1. **Conceptual analysis:** necessary and sufficient conditions; the classical model and its challengers.
2. **Thought experiments and intuition pumps:** from Gettier cases to trolley problems; when intuition is evidence and when it is noise.
3. **Formalization:** predicate logic, modal notation ($\Box$, $\Diamond$), probability; when formalizing clarifies and when it obfuscates.
4. **Reflective equilibrium:** wide vs. narrow; the coherentist method of ethics generalized.
5. **Genealogy and debunking explanations:** explaining the origin of a belief to test its authority.
6. **Phenomenology, ordinary language, and experimental philosophy:** description, use, and survey data as methods.
7. **Negative capability:** the technique of *dissolving* a problem rather than solving it.

### Chapter 4 — How to Read, Write, and Evaluate Philosophical Work
1. The anatomy of a philosophical paper: question, thesis, argument, objection, reply.
2. A taxonomy of fallacies — formal, informal, and *dialectical* (straw man, equivocation, begging the question, motte-and-bailey, deepity).
3. The criteria of a good theory: consistency, fertility, simplicity, conservatism, explanatory power, adequacy to the data of ordinary thought.
4. The ethics of intellectual discourse: honesty about strength of evidence, calibrated confidence, the vice of cleverness.
5. Self-examination: the *philosophical diary* and the habit of writing out one's own best objection.

**A closing note on the book's ambition:** Russell wrote his little volume in 1912 to show that philosophy enlarges the mind and that its questions are worth the effort. A century on, the questions have multiplied, the tools have sharpened, and the honest state of the discipline is more mixed than he allowed. This book tries to say what we know, what we merely believe, what we have learned to reframe, and what remains stubbornly open — and to give the reader, in every chapter, the instruments to continue the work independently.
